How LaneLock scores a draft

When your fifth lane locks in, LaneLock compares your team to the enemy's in three layers: each lane on its own, the bot lane as a duo, and the two team comps as a whole. Here's what goes into each one.

1. Each lane, one matchup at a time

Top, jungle and mid are scored as one-on-one matchups between your champion and the enemy in the same role.

Real ranked win rates come first

For most popular matchups LaneLock uses real data: how often one champion beats the other in that lane in Emerald+ ranked solo queue on the current patch (from LoLalytics, patch 26.22). If one top laner wins 54% of their games against another, that 4-point edge counts toward your score. Every lane on the results page links to the full head-to-head on LoLalytics if you want to dig deeper.

Small samples are treated with suspicion. A matchup played 300 times can swing a few points on luck alone, so LaneLock shrinks the edge toward even until there are enough games to trust it. A pairing with tens of thousands of games keeps nearly all of its edge, and one with a few hundred keeps less than half.

Tier lists and champion classes fill the gaps

Some pairings are too rare to have reliable data, especially with off-meta picks. For those, LaneLock falls back to a model built from three things:

  • Lane tiers. Each champion has a tier in each role (S+ down to D) from public patch tier lists. Champions that are playable in a role but rarely ranked there are marked "niche". A champion rolled outside their usual roles takes a penalty.
  • Class matchups. Every champion belongs to a class, such as juggernaut, diver, assassin, burst mage, artillery mage or marksman. Some classes have a natural edge over others: an assassin can jump on an immobile artillery mage, while a juggernaut walks through a squishy duelist who can't kite.
  • Known counters. A short list of famous lane counters adds a bigger bump when they show up.

In the jungle, early-game strength also matters, because a jungler who wins early skirmishes can snowball the whole map.

Even when real data exists, the tier list still adds a small amount, since a champion who is strong on the patch overall tends to do better in any given game.

2. Bot lane is scored as a duo

Your ADC and support are judged together, because that's how bot lane is actually played. Their individual matchups still count, but three extra things are layered on top:

  • How well the pair fits. Each ADC has a style (late-game hypercarry, all-in, utility or poke) and each support has a style (engage, enchanter, warden or poke). A hypercarry with an enchanter who shields and heals them is a classic fit. Two poke champions with nobody to start a fight is not.
  • Which duo wins the lane. An all-in duo punishes a scaling duo before it comes online, and a poke duo can chip a scaling duo out of lane.
  • Cross matchups. Your support fights their ADC as much as your ADC does. A hook support against an immobile marksman is a nightmare for the marksman.

3. The team comps

Once the lanes are scored, LaneLock looks at each five-champion team as a whole.

  • Damage mix. A team doing almost all physical or almost all magic damage is easy to itemize against, so it loses points.
  • Frontline, engage and crowd control. Teams need someone to soak damage, someone to start fights and enough stuns and slows to make them stick.
  • Protecting the carry. A late-game marksman with no peel around them gets punished. One with plenty of peel gets a bonus.
  • Power curve. Early-game champions push the result toward the first half of the game, late-game champions toward the second.
  • Champion combos. Well-known pairings across lanes, such as a wombo-combo engage followed by area damage, add a little extra.

Hard engage closes the gap before poke can wear a team down. Poke chips away at a backline before a protect comp can fight. Peel and disengage stop a dive cold. A pick comp catches a split pusher alone, and side-lane pressure pulls a teamfight comp apart.

Putting it together

The lane results make up the early game. Jungle and mid count a little more than top, because they move around the map, and the bot duo counts the most:

Top1.00

Mid1.15
Bot lane (ADC and support)1.20

The team comp results make up the late game. The early and late game are added together into one number, which becomes your win chance. That's why a draft can lose two lanes and still win if its comp is much stronger, or win every lane and still throw.

Win chance never goes below 3% or above 97%, because no draft is ever a sure thing. Luck only decides a game when the two drafts are almost exactly even. The play-by-play, kills and gold graph are generated to match the result, so they tell the story of why you won or lost.

Tips for better drafts

  • Lock your best counter first. Every lock rerolls the other lanes, so grab a great matchup as soon as you see it.
  • Think about bot lane as a pair. A strong ADC roll is worth more if you can still find a support that fits them.
  • Watch your damage mix. If you already have three magic damage champions, lean toward physical damage for the last lanes.
  • Don't forget a frontline. A team of five squishy carries struggles even if every lane is won.
  • Read the results page. It shows which rolls you passed on would have done better, and why.

Limits

LaneLock is a game, not a coaching tool. Real matches depend on players, runes, items and a hundred decisions the model can't see. The data reflects one patch and one skill bracket, and the comp model is a simplification of how pros think about drafts. It should still reward the kind of drafting that wins real games.
